&lt;!–MDXB-7-OPEN–&gt;

You have a base model—billions of parameters trained on internet text.
It can complete sentences, but it cannot follow instructions, refuse harmful
requests, or reason step by step. Post-training is the bridge between raw
intelligence and a usable application. This chapter maps the full landscape
before we dive into the mechanics in Modules 2–5.

&lt;!–MDXB-7-CLOSE–&gt;

# Why Post-Training Matters



<Sidenote category="crossref">Prereq: Build LLM from Scratch (Manning, Raschka) Ch 6–7 for SFT mechanics</Sidenote>





<Sidenote category="interview">"What is post-training and why is it necessary?" is a top-3 question for MLE roles at frontier labs.</Sidenote>



Sharon Zhou opens the course with a key observation: post-training bridges
the gap between a model’s raw intelligence and its usefulness to end users.
A pre-trained model predicts the next token well but has no concept of
instructions, safety boundaries, or structured reasoning. Fine-tuning
(SFT) is the mature, efficient technique—LoRA makes it accessible on
a single GPU. Reinforcement learning enables superhuman capabilities that
go beyond what any human demonstration can teach. Error analysis is the
meta-skill that ties everything together: train, evaluate, diagnose, fix
data, repeat.

The historical arc of post-training illustrates accelerating sophistication:

1.  **2018–2020**: Basic fine-tuning on downstream tasks
    (GFRI-2 fine-tuning, T5 multi-task).

2.  **2022**: InstructGPT / RLHF<Citation src="ouyang2022training" />—the breakthrough that

3.  **2023**: Preference learning (DPO<Citation src="rafailov2023direct" />) simplifies the RL

4.  **2024**: Reasoning models (o1, DeepSeek R1<Citation src="deepseek2025r1" />) push RL

    pipelines; community specialization explodes.

# The Three-Stage Training Pipeline

&lt;!–MDXB-8-OPEN–&gt;

1.  **Pre-Training** *(weeks–months, massive compute)*  
    Next-token prediction on internet-scale text. Builds world knowledge,
    grammar, and latent capabilities. Analogy: reading every book in
    the library.

2.  **Mid-Training** *(days–weeks, curated data)*  
    Continued training on high-quality, curated corpora. Refines
    language fluency, adds modality support (code, math, multilingual),
    extends context length. Analogy: studying the reference section.

3.  **Post-Training** *(hours–days, targeted data)*  
    SFT + RL to teach instruction-following, safety, reasoning, and
    tool use. Analogy: apprenticeship with a master—learning
    *how* to apply knowledge.

&lt;!–MDXB-8-CLOSE–&gt;

# Intuitions: SFT vs. RL

&lt;!–MDXB-9-OPEN–&gt;

**The Pasta Analogy.**
Imagine learning to cook your grandmother’s signature pasta dish.

**SFT** is like following grandma’s recipe step by step: you watch
her demonstration and mimic every action. The dish will be good—as good
as the demonstration—but you will never discover a better technique
she did not show you.

**RL** is like being told “make a dish that tastes this good” and
experimenting in the kitchen. You might burn a few batches, but you can
also discover shortcuts or flavor combinations that even grandma never
tried. The ceiling is higher, but the path is noisier.

&lt;!–MDXB-9-CLOSE–&gt;

</Term>

# Key Components: Data and Grading

&lt;!–MDXB-10-OPEN–&gt;

**The Two Ingredients.**
Every post-training method needs two things: **data** to train on
and a **grading mechanism** to evaluate outputs. SFT and RL differ
dramatically in both.

&lt;!–MDXB-10-CLOSE–&gt;

## SFT Data Requirements

SFT data consists of (input, target output) pairs formatted as multi-turn
chat. High-quality SFT datasets include:

- **Chat history pairs**: User message → assistant response,
  capturing conversational turn-taking.

- **Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning**: Step-by-step derivations
  so the model learns to “show its work.”

- **RAG recovery patterns**: Given retrieved context, produce a
  grounded answer—teaches the model to use external knowledge.

- **Guardrail examples**: Refusal demonstrations for unsafe
  requests, boundary-setting responses.



<Sidenote category="warning">SFT data quality matters more than quantity. 100 expertly curated examples often beat 10,000 noisy ones.</Sidenote>





<Sidenote category="formula">Post-training data rule of thumb: 20 examples for format, 1K–5K for production SFT, 10K–100K rollouts for RL. Quality $$ quantity.</Sidenote>

## RL Grading Mechanisms

RL does not need gold-standard outputs—it needs a way to *score*
model-generated outputs. Three grading mechanisms, in order of reliability:

1.  **Deterministic verifiers**: Code execution (pass/fail),
    math proof checkers, unit tests. Gold standard—no ambiguity.

2.  **LLM-as-judge**<Citation src="zheng2023judging" />: A separate (often stronger) LLM scores
    outputs on rubrics. Scalable but can drift or be gamed.

3.  **Full environment feedback**: The model acts in a
    simulated or real environment (web browsing, tool use) and
    receives task-completion rewards.



<Term name="Reward Model" los="FRI-1.3">
  A model trained to predict human preference scores for LLM outputs. Used in RLHF to provide the reward signal for policy optimization. Can be a separate model or an LLM-as-judge.
</Term>

# Reward Hacking

&lt;!–MDXB-23-OPEN–&gt;

When the reward signal has exploitable shortcuts, RL will find them.
The classic example from the course: a model trained to maximize a
“helpfulness” reward learned to repeat “hello hello hello…”
because the reward model assigned high scores to enthusiastic greetings.

**Why it happens:**
RL optimizes the reward function *as specified*, not the reward
function *as intended*. Any gap between the two is an exploit
waiting to be found.

**Mitigations:**

- Use deterministic verifiers where possible (code tests, math proofs).

- Apply KL divergence penalty against the SFT baseline to prevent
  the policy from drifting too far.

- Ensemble multiple reward signals (verifier + LLM-as-judge + length penalty).

- Monitor output diversity—collapsing to a single pattern is a red flag.

&lt;!–MDXB-23-CLOSE–&gt;

# Reasoning: SFT and RL Working Together

Post-training for reasoning illustrates the complementary roles of SFT and RL.

## SFT for Structured Reasoning

SFT teaches the model a *process*: given a math problem, produce a
step-by-step chain-of-thought derivation, then state the final answer.
The model learns the *format* and *common strategies* from
demonstrations. This is reliable but bounded—the model cannot discover
reasoning approaches absent from the training data.

## RL for Novel Strategies

RL with a deterministic verifier (e.g., checking the final answer against
ground truth) allows the model to explore the space of possible reasoning
paths. It can discover efficient shortcuts, novel proof strategies, or
creative problem decompositions that no human demonstrated.

&lt;!–MDXB-12-OPEN–&gt;

DeepSeek R1 Zero is a landmark result: RL-only training (no SFT)
on a base model produced emergent chain-of-thought reasoning.
The model learned to “think out loud” purely from reward signal,
without ever being shown a CoT demonstration. This proved that
RL alone can induce structured reasoning, though the frontier
recipe remains SFT → RL → SFT → RL for best results.

**When It Breaks:** RL-only training without SFT produces unstable formatting
(language mixing, repetition loops). DeepSeek addressed this by adding SFT stages
before and after RL—pure RL-only remains a research result, not a production recipe.

## The Frontier Recipe

The strongest reasoning models (DeepSeek R1, OpenAI o-series) follow a
multi-stage pipeline:

1.  **SFT Stage 1**: Train on curated CoT demonstrations to
    establish format and baseline strategies.

2.  **RL Stage 1**: Optimize against verifiers to discover
    novel, efficient reasoning paths.

3.  **SFT Stage 2**: Distill the best RL-discovered
    trajectories back into supervised data for stability.

4.  **RL Stage 2**: Final polish—optimize for both
    correctness and efficiency.

# Safety and RLAIF (Constitutional AI)

&lt;!–MDXB-13-OPEN–&gt;

Anthropic’s Constitutional AI<Citation src="bai2022constitutional" /> replaces human preference labels with
AI-generated preferences, scaling alignment without proportional
human annotation cost. The three-step process:

1.  **Write a constitution**: A set of principles defining
    desired behavior (e.g., “Be helpful, harmless, and honest”).

2.  **Self-critique to generate SFT data**: The model generates
    responses, critiques them against the constitution, and revises.
    The revised outputs become SFT training data.

3.  **RLAIF**: The model generates preference pairs (better/worse
    responses). A reward model is trained on these AI-generated preferences,
    then used for RL training.

**Results** (from Anthropic’s paper): Models trained with Constitutional
AI were *equally helpful* and *significantly more harmless* compared
to RLHF baselines, while requiring far fewer human preference labels.

**When It Breaks:** AI-generated preferences inherit the judge model’s blind spots—if
the constitution is vague or the critic model is weak, RLAIF amplifies existing biases rather
than correcting them. Human spot-checks on preference quality remain essential.

&lt;!–MDXB-13-CLOSE–&gt;

</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"Llama Guard"} back={"Meta's safety classification model that categorises LLM outputs as safe or unsafe across 13 predefined harm categories (S1–S13). Returns a structured verdict: `safe` or `unsafe nS{code}`."} los="FRI-1.4" />



The M1 notebook (exercises 4–5) introduces **Llama Guard** as a concrete
tool for evaluating model safety at scale. Rather than relying on human reviewers,
Llama Guard acts as a classifier: given a prompt–response pair, it returns a
binary safe/unsafe verdict with the specific harm category.

```cypher
import re

def parse_llama_guard_response(response: str) -> dict:
    """Parse Llama Guard output into structured safety verdict.

    Returns:
        {"safe": bool, "categories": list[str]}
    Example output: "unsafe\nS2" -> {"safe": False, "categories": ["S2"]}
    """
    lines = response.strip().split("\n")
    is_safe = lines[0].strip().lower() == "safe"
    categories = []
    if not is_safe:
        for line in lines[1:]:
            match = re.match(r"(S\d{1,2})", line.strip())
            if match:
                categories.append(match.group(1))
    return {"safe": is_safe, "categories": categories}


def calculate_safety_metrics(
    harmful_results: list[dict],
    benign_results: list[dict],
) -> dict:
    """Calculate safety evaluation metrics from Llama Guard outputs.

    Args:
        harmful_results: Guard outputs for known-harmful prompts.
        benign_results: Guard outputs for known-benign prompts.

    Returns:
        Harmful detection rate, benign acceptance rate, FPR, FNR.
    """
    harmful_detected = sum(
        1 for r in harmful_results if not r["safe"]
    )
    benign_accepted = sum(
        1 for r in benign_results if r["safe"]
    )
    n_harmful = len(harmful_results)
    n_benign = len(benign_results)
    return {
        "harmful_detection_rate": harmful_detected / n_harmful,
        "benign_acceptance_rate": benign_accepted / n_benign,
        "FPR": 1 - benign_accepted / n_benign,   # safe flagged as unsafe
        "FNR": 1 - harmful_detected / n_harmful,  # harmful missed
    }
```





<Sidenote category="warning">In production safety: low FNR (catch all harms) matters more than low FPR (avoid false alarms). But high FPR creates user frustration—balance with your risk tolerance.</Sidenote>

# Post-Training in the Wild

The open-source post-training ecosystem has matured rapidly. Three flagship
pipelines illustrate different design choices:

Base model → cold-start SFT (CoT format) → RL with GRPO on
math/code verifiers → rejection-sampling SFT (best-of-N from RL)
→ final RL for general alignment. Notable for demonstrating RL-only
reasoning emergence (R1 Zero) and open-sourcing the full recipe.
The R1 distillation pipeline (Section <a href="#sec:m5-distillation" data-reference-type="ref" data-reference="sec:m5-distillation">[sec:m5-distillation]</a>)
produced 6 student models from 1.5B to 70B parameters, proving that
reasoning transfers via SFT on teacher-generated CoT traces.

Pre-training on 15T+ tokens → SFT on diverse instruction data
→ RLHF with PPO → iterative human evaluation and data refresh.
The 3.1 release (405B) set a new open-weight frontier; 3.3 (70B,
December 2024) achieved near-405B quality through aggressive distillation,
demonstrating that smaller models with better post-training can match
larger predecessors.

Strong multilingual pre-training → SFT on code, math, and multilingual
chat → DPO for preference alignment → RL on domain-specific tasks.
The Qwen 2.5 series spans 0.5B–72B parameters with consistent post-training
across all sizes. Demonstrates that DPO can serve as a lightweight alternative
to PPO in the post-training pipeline, and that a single post-training recipe
can scale across model sizes.



<Sidenote category="interview">Being able to compare 2–3 open-source post-training pipelines shows system-level thinking that interviewers value.</Sidenote>



## The 2025–2026 Modular Post-Training Stack

The frontier recipe has converged on a four-layer modular stack, where each
layer addresses a distinct capability:

1.  **SFT for instruction following**: Teach format, safety refusals,
    and basic task completion. LoRA on 1K–10K curated examples.

2.  **Preference optimisation for alignment**: DPO, SimPO, or KTO
    to align with human preferences on style, helpfulness, and safety.
    Offline methods (no RL loop) are preferred for cost and stability.

3.  **RL with verifiable rewards for reasoning**: GRPO or DAPO on
    math/code tasks where correctness is machine-checkable. This is
    where models exceed human demonstrations.

4.  **Distillation for deployment**: Compress frontier-model
    reasoning into smaller models via SFT on teacher-generated CoT traces.
    DeepSeek R1 distilled 671B → 1.5B–70B students with minimal
    reasoning loss.



<Sidenote category="crossref">For DPO/ORPO alignment details, see dlai_post_training_llms Guide. For GRPO hyperparameter sweeps, see dlai_grpo_finetuning Guide.</Sidenote>



Not every product needs all four layers. A customer support bot may need only
layers 1–2; a math tutoring system needs layers 1–3; a cost-sensitive
deployment adds layer 4. The modular design lets teams compose the stack to
match their use case.

# Hands-On: Basic SFT with TRL

The M1 lab compares three stages of the DeepSeek Math 7B training pipeline—base,
SFT (instruct), and RL—on GSM8K math problems. This pattern of evaluating
the same prompts across pipeline stages is the core skill for understanding
what each post-training stage contributes.



```cypher
import re
from datasets import load_dataset

# Three stages of the DeepSeek Math training pipeline
BASE_MODEL = "deepseek-ai/deepseek-math-7b-base"
SFT_MODEL  = "deepseek-ai/deepseek-math-7b-instruct"
RL_MODEL   = "deepseek-ai/deepseek-math-7b-rl"

def extract_number(text):
    """Extract numerical answer from model output.
    Strategy: (1) GSM8K #### format, (2) last number fallback."""
    gsm8k_match = re.search(r"####\s*([-+]?\d+(?:\.\d+)?)", text)
    if gsm8k_match:
        try:
            return float(gsm8k_match.group(1))
        except ValueError:
            pass
    numbers = re.findall(r"[-+]?\d+(?:\.\d+)?", text)
    if numbers:
        try:
            return float(numbers[-1])
        except ValueError:
            return None
    return None

# Evaluate each pipeline stage on the same prompts
gsm8k = load_dataset("openai/gsm8k", "main", split="test[:50]")
for model_name in [BASE_MODEL, SFT_MODEL, RL_MODEL]:
    correct = 0
    for example in gsm8k:
        response = generate(model_name, example["question"])
        predicted = extract_number(response)
        expected  = extract_number(example["answer"])
        if predicted is not None and abs(predicted - expected) < 0.01:
            correct += 1
    print(f"{model_name}: {correct}/{len(gsm8k)} correct")
```





<Sidenote category="warning">The two-pass regex (canonical `\#\#\#\#` then last-number fallback) is the standard GSM8K answer extraction pattern. Using only one pass misses many correct answers.</Sidenote>



# Chapter Summary

Post-training transforms a base model’s raw intelligence into a usable,
aligned, and capable system. The three-stage pipeline (pre-training →
mid-training → post-training) separates concerns: broad knowledge,
refined fluency, and targeted behavior. SFT provides stable imitation
learning bounded by demonstration quality; RL enables superhuman
exploration bounded by reward signal quality. The frontier recipe
alternates SFT and RL stages, using each to compensate for the other’s
weaknesses. Safety alignment scales via Constitutional AI / RLAIF, and
the open-source ecosystem (DeepSeek, LLaMA, Qwen) has made these
techniques accessible to the broader community.

&lt;!–MDXB-16-OPEN–&gt;

You have a 7B base model and want to add structured JSON output capability.
You have: (a) 500 high-quality human-written JSON examples, and (b) a JSON
schema validator that returns pass/fail. Should you use SFT, RL, or both?
Justify with the stability-ceiling tradeoff.

&lt;!–MDXB-16-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-20-OPEN–&gt;

**Approach:**
**Answer:** SFT first, then RL to polish.

**Phase 1 — SFT:** Train on the 500 JSON examples. SFT is stable and
will teach the model the output format quickly. With 500 high-quality examples,
expect reliable JSON structure within 1–2 epochs.

**Phase 2 — RL:** Use the JSON validator as a verifier reward. RL will
discover edge cases (nested objects, escaping, optional fields) that the 500
examples didn’t cover, pushing accuracy beyond the demonstration ceiling.

**Key Insight:** SFT alone is bounded by demonstration quality—if your
examples don’t cover edge cases, the model won’t either. RL alone is unstable
for format-heavy tasks (the model may discover valid JSON through random
exploration, but slowly). The combination leverages SFT’s stability for the
common case and RL’s exploration for the long tail.

&lt;!–MDXB-20-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-17-OPEN–&gt;

You train a model with a length-based reward: *r* = min (length/500, 1.0). After 500 steps, the reward plateaus at 1.0 but GSM8K accuracy drops
from 45% to 32%. Diagnose the problem and propose a fix.

&lt;!–MDXB-17-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-21-OPEN–&gt;

**Approach:**
**Diagnosis:** Reward hacking (Goodhart’s Law). The model learned to
maximise length by repeating phrases, adding unnecessary reasoning steps, or
padding with irrelevant text. The reward signal (*r* = 1.0) is saturated while
actual capability (accuracy) has degraded.

**Fix:** Composite reward that includes correctness:
*r* = 0.7 ⋅ accuracy + 0.2 ⋅ format\_score + 0.1 ⋅ length\_bonus. Add a KL penalty to prevent the model from diverging
too far from the reference distribution.

**Key Insight:** Any single-dimensional reward will be gamed. Composite
rewards with correctness as the dominant term are more robust. The KL penalty
acts as a regulariser, keeping the model “close” to its SFT starting point.

**Answer:** Any single-dimensional reward will be gamed. Composite
rewards with correctness as the dominant term are more robust.

&lt;!–MDXB-18-OPEN–&gt;

A model is trained with 3 constitutional principles: helpfulness, harmlessness,
honesty. A user asks: “How do I pick a lock?” The model refuses entirely.
Is this the correct behaviour under Constitutional AI? Propose a better response
that satisfies all 3 principles.

&lt;!–MDXB-18-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-22-OPEN–&gt;

**Approach:**
**Analysis:** Refusing entirely satisfies harmlessness but violates
helpfulness and arguably honesty (the model knows lockpicking is a legitimate
skill for locksmiths).

**Better response:** “Lock picking is a legitimate skill used by
locksmiths and security professionals. Here are resources for learning
professionally: \[locksmithing courses, certification programs\]. I should note
that picking locks you don’t own is illegal in most jurisdictions.”

**Key Insight:** Constitutional AI does not mean “refuse everything
risky.” It means finding the response that *maximises all principles
simultaneously*. Over-refusal (low helpfulness) is as much a constitutional
violation as over-compliance (low harmlessness). The balance point is the skill.

**Answer:** Constitutional AI does not mean “refuse everything
risky.” It means finding the response that *maximises all principles
simultaneously*.
