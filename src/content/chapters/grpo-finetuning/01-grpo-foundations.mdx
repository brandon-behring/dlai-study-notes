&lt;!–MDXB-8-OPEN–&gt;

This chapter establishes why reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) exists and what
makes GRPO different from prior RL algorithms. We cover the SFT–RLHF–DPO–GRPO
progression, map RL concepts onto LLM training, and introduce Wordle as the
running example that threads through the entire course. Every concept here feeds
directly into reward design (Chapter 2) and loss computation (Chapter 3).

&lt;!–MDXB-8-CLOSE–&gt;

# From SFT to Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

&lt;!–MDXB-9-OPEN–&gt;

**What**: Supervised fine-tuning teaches an LLM by showing it labeled
(prompt, response) pairs. The model learns to *imitate* demonstrations.  
**Why**: SFT works well with thousands of high-quality examples but
cannot teach entirely new reasoning strategies. It copies patterns rather
than discovering better solutions.

&lt;!–MDXB-9-CLOSE–&gt;



<Sidenote category="interview">Choose SFT over RL fine-tuning when you have 1k+ labeled examples and the task does not benefit from chain-of-thought reasoning.</Sidenote>



The SFT training loop is straightforward: forward pass generates output,
backward pass computes error against the ground-truth response, weight update
reduces that error. Repeating across thousands of examples teaches the
desired behavior. However, SFT has two structural limitations:

- **Data hunger**: typically requires thousands of high-quality labeled
  examples to produce meaningful quality improvements

- **Overfitting risk**: the model memorizes training patterns and
  degrades on unseen inputs, especially with smaller datasets



<Sidenote category="warning">SFT with chain-of-thought data teaches the model to *format* reasoning traces, not to *reason*. It mimics structure without discovering better strategies.</Sidenote>



Reinforcement learning offers a fundamentally different approach: the model
learns by *experimenting* and receiving feedback, rather than mimicking
fixed examples. The RL mapping for LLMs is direct:

- **Agent**: the LLM

- **Observation**: the input prompt from the environment

- **Action**: the generated token sequence (response)

- **Reward**: a numerical score from an evaluation function



<Sidenote category="pattern">RL mental model: puppy (agent) learns to fetch (action) by receiving treats (reward). LLM generates better responses by receiving higher scores.</Sidenote>



# From RLHF to DPO to GRPO

&lt;!–MDXB-12-OPEN–&gt;

**Core Insight:** Each generation of RL algorithms for LLMs reduces
the human annotation burden: RLHF needs full rankings, DPO needs pairwise
preferences, and GRPO needs only a programmable reward function with zero
human labels.

**Why It Matters:** GRPO enables reinforcement fine-tuning for tasks
where human labels are scarce or expensive—you only need to define
verifiable success criteria in code.

**When It Breaks:** GRPO requires that outputs can be scored
programmatically. For subjective tasks (creative writing, tone),
you may still need DPO/RLHF or LLM-as-a-judge reward functions.

&lt;!–MDXB-12-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-15-OPEN–&gt;

**Why interviewers ask this:** Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind
research-engineer and post-training team interviews (L5/L6) ask “walk me
through how LLM alignment has evolved.” This is a gating question for any
role touching fine-tuning, RLHF infrastructure, or model alignment. DeepSeek’s
R1 paper made GRPO a top-of-mind topic for 2025–2026 hiring cycles.

**What they’re really testing:** Whether you understand the
*progression*—each algorithm reduces human annotation burden while
maintaining alignment quality. The impressive answer explains what each
method eliminates (RLHF: nothing; DPO: reward model; GRPO: human labels
entirely) and states when you would choose each.

**Expected depth by level:**

- **IC3/IC4:** Name RLHF, DPO, and GRPO. State what each
  eliminates. Know that GRPO uses group-relative advantages
  instead of a critic network.

- **IC5+/Staff:** Discuss GRPO’s connection to PPO (clipping,
  KL penalty), explain why GRPO scales to reasoning tasks
  (verifiable rewards), and analyze the trade-off between
  programmatic rewards and LLM-as-judge for subjective tasks.

**Red flag:** Saying “GRPO replaces RLHF entirely.” GRPO requires
verifiable rewards—it cannot handle subjective quality (tone, creativity)
where human preferences remain essential.

&lt;!–MDXB-15-CLOSE–&gt;

**RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback) powers
ChatGPT<Citation src="christiano2017rlhf" /><Citation src="ouyang2022instructgpt" />.
Its four-step workflow: (1) sample multiple responses, (2) annotators rank them,
(3) train a separate reward model on preferences, (4) fine-tune the LLM with
PPO<Citation src="schulman2017ppo" /> using reward model scores. This requires multiple
model copies in memory and extensive human annotation effort.

**DPO** (Direct Preference Optimization) simplifies by skipping the reward
model<Citation src="rafailov2023dpo" />. It trains directly on pairwise preference data:
for each prompt, increase the probability of the chosen response and decrease
the rejected one. Still requires large numbers of annotated comparisons.



<Sidenote category="formula">DPO objective: increase $P(chosen)$, decrease $P(rejected)$ for each preference pair. No reward model needed.</Sidenote>





<Sidenote category="crossref">For DPO foundations and preference data curation, see Post-Training LLMs Ch 3</Sidenote>



**GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization) eliminates human labels
entirely<Citation src="shao2024grpo" />. Developed by the DeepSeek team for
DeepSeek R1<Citation src="deepseek2025r1" />, its three-step loop:

1.  Send a prompt to the LLM and sample *multiple* candidate responses

2.  Score each response with one or more *programmable reward functions*

3.  Push up the probability of above-average responses; push down below-average



```cypher
# GRPO conceptual pseudocode
for prompt in training_data:
    responses = llm.sample(prompt, n=16, temperature=0.7)
    rewards = [reward_fn(prompt, r) for r in responses]
    advantages = normalize(rewards)  # mean=0, std=1
    # Push up above-average, push down below-average
    update_weights(responses, advantages)
```





<Sidenote category="interview">GRPO replaces the learned critic (value model) with group-relative scoring—normalize rewards within each prompt's batch.</Sidenote>



# When to Use Reinforcement Fine-Tuning

&lt;!–MDXB-10-OPEN–&gt;

**Decision: SFT vs. RFT**

**Use SFT when:**

- You have ample labeled data (100k+ rows)

- The task is pattern matching, not multi-step reasoning

**Use RFT (GRPO) when:**

- No labeled data, but outputs are verifiable (code, math)

- Limited labels (&lt;1k examples) and chain-of-thought improves performance

- The task requires multi-step reasoning or iterative refinement

**Use DPO/RLHF when:**

- The task is non-verifiable and subjective (style, tone)

- You can collect pairwise human preference labels

**Decision Rule:** Check three gates—labeled data volume, chain-of-thought
benefit, and output verifiability. If verifiable + CoT helps, GRPO is the
strongest option regardless of label count.

&lt;!–MDXB-10-CLOSE–&gt;



<Sidenote category="pattern">RFT sweet-spot tasks: math problem solving, code generation and debugging, agentic multi-step workflows. All share verifiable outputs and CoT benefit.</Sidenote>



Predibase demonstrated this with Triton kernel generation: a GRPO-tuned
Qwen 2.5-7B outperformed Claude 3.7 Sonnet thinking, DeepSeek R1, and
OpenAI o1 on translating PyTorch code into optimized Triton kernels.
GRPO works with as few as ten examples, scaling as you increase prompts.

&lt;!–MDXB-13-OPEN–&gt;

**Core Insight:** Unlike SFT, which teaches the model to reproduce
fixed reasoning traces, GRPO lets the model *discover* better
reasoning strategies by rewarding outcomes rather than prescribing steps.

**Why It Matters:** The model’s internal chain of thought evolves during
training. It learns which reasoning patterns lead to higher rewards and
self-refines, producing strategies not present in any training data.

**When It Breaks:** If the reward function does not capture the
true objective (reward hacking), the model discovers strategies that
maximize score without solving the actual problem. See Chapter 2.

# The Wordle Challenge

Wordle provides an ideal sandbox for GRPO because it requires planning,
hypothesis testing, and step-by-step reasoning—exactly the skills
reinforcement fine-tuning strengthens.



<Sidenote category="warning">The base Qwen 2.5-7B model solves 0/10 Wordle games. After GRPO tuning it solves 3/10. After SFT + GRPO it solves 7/10.</Sidenote>



**Game rules**: Guess a secret five-letter word in at most six tries.
After each guess, every letter receives feedback:

- **Green** (): correct letter, correct position

- **Yellow** (–): letter in the word, wrong position

- **Gray** (×): letter not in the word at all

The LLM receives a system prompt with game rules, feedback format, an example
game, and instructions to use tags for chain-of-thought
reasoning<Citation src="wei2022cot" /> before outputting a guess in tags.



```cypher
# Core game loop for Wordle evaluation
def next_turn(past_guesses, secret_word, adapter_id=None):
    prompt = render_prompt(past_guesses)
    response = generate(prompt, adapter_id=adapter_id)
    guess = extract_guess(response)  # regex on <guess> tags
    feedback = get_feedback(guess, secret_word)
    past_guesses.append(GuessWithFeedback(guess, feedback))
    return past_guesses
```





<Sidenote category="practice">Try running both base and fine-tuned models on the same secret word. Compare reasoning depth in the `<think>` tags.</Sidenote>



The base model makes critical errors: it concludes letters are absent when
they are present, repeats prior guesses, and takes random guesses instead
of building on feedback. The GRPO-tuned model analyzes feedback systematically,
eliminates impossible words, considers vowel coverage, and verifies its guess
satisfies all prior constraints before committing.

&lt;!–MDXB-14-OPEN–&gt;

**Core Insight:** Because the model emits its reasoning in
tags before the guess, GRPO indirectly trains the
chain-of-thought itself. Reasoning patterns that lead to correct guesses
get reinforced; sloppy reasoning that leads to wrong guesses gets suppressed.

**Why It Matters:** You do not need to label reasoning quality directly.
The outcome reward propagates back to improve the reasoning process itself.

**When It Breaks:** If the reward ignores intermediate reasoning quality,
the model may develop correct-but-fragile shortcuts that fail on harder inputs.

You are building a system that translates natural language specifications into
Python functions. You have 50 specification-function pairs as examples, plus
a test suite of unit tests for each specification. The model needs to produce
code that passes the tests. Which fine-tuning approach (SFT, DPO, RLHF, GRPO)
is most appropriate, and why?

&lt;!–MDXB-16-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-19-OPEN–&gt;

**Approach:** Evaluate against the three RFT decision gates.

**Step 1: Label volume check**  
50 labeled examples is far below the SFT threshold of 1k+. SFT alone
would likely overfit.

**Step 2: Chain-of-thought benefit**  
Code generation benefits significantly from step-by-step reasoning:
planning the function signature, decomposing the logic, handling edge cases.

**Step 3: Verifiability**  
Unit tests provide a fully automated, objective scoring mechanism. No human
judgment needed.

**Answer:** **GRPO** is the strongest choice. The reward function
runs the generated code against unit tests and returns a score (e.g., fraction
of tests passed). All three gates favor GRPO: limited labels, CoT benefit,
and fully verifiable outputs.

**Key Insight:** The combination of SFT warmup (using the 50 examples
to teach output format) followed by GRPO (to optimize test pass rate) would
likely outperform either method alone, mirroring the Wordle SFT+GRPO strategy.

**Approach:** **RL Mapping:**

- **Agent**: the LLM being fine-tuned

- **Environment**: the database schema + execution engine

- **Observation**: the natural language question + schema context

- **Action**: the generated SQL query (token sequence)

- **Reward**: execution result score (e.g., 1.0 if query returns
  correct rows, 0.5 for correct columns but wrong filter, 0.0 for
  syntax error)

**GRPO group for one step:** Given a single NL question, the LLM generates
*N* candidate SQL queries (e.g., *N* = 16) using temperature sampling. Each query
is executed against the database and scored. The rewards are normalized
within this group: queries that return correct results get positive advantages,
while incorrect or syntactically invalid queries get negative advantages.

**Key Insight:** The “group-relative” aspect means GRPO does not need
an absolute notion of good vs. bad. It only needs *variation* within the
group. If all 16 queries are equally wrong, advantages are zero and no learning
occurs. Temperature control is critical for generating diverse candidates.

&lt;!–MDXB-18-OPEN–&gt;

A startup has three candidate tasks for reinforcement fine-tuning. For each,
determine whether it is a good fit for GRPO and justify using the three RFT
gates (label volume, chain-of-thought benefit, output verifiability):

(a) **Legal clause extraction**: Given a contract PDF, extract all
indemnification clauses. The team has 5,000 labeled contracts and a regex-based
extraction pipeline that serves as ground truth.

(b) **Creative marketing copy**: Generate social media posts for product
launches. The team has 50 examples of posts the CMO liked but no objective
quality metric.

(c) **Multi-step math tutoring**: Given a student’s incorrect solution,
generate a Socratic dialogue that guides them to the right answer. The team has
30 example dialogues and can verify the final student answer with a symbolic
math solver.

&lt;!–MDXB-18-CLOSE–&gt;

&lt;!–MDXB-21-OPEN–&gt;

**Approach:** Evaluate each task against the three RFT decision gates.

**(a) Legal clause extraction:**

- **Label volume**: 5,000 labeled examples is ample for SFT.

- **CoT benefit**: Extraction is pattern matching, not multi-step reasoning.

- **Verifiability**: Yes (regex ground truth), but SFT already has enough data.

**Verdict: SFT.** With abundant labeled data and a pattern-matching task,
SFT is simpler and sufficient. GRPO adds unnecessary complexity.

**(b) Creative marketing copy:**

- **Label volume**: Only 50 examples—too few for SFT.

- **CoT benefit**: Moderate (brainstorming process helps).

- **Verifiability**: No objective metric—quality is subjective.

**Verdict: DPO.** The output is non-verifiable and subjective. Collect
pairwise preferences from the CMO and use DPO. GRPO cannot work without a
programmatic reward function.

**(c) Multi-step math tutoring:**

- **Label volume**: Only 30 examples—far below SFT threshold.

- **CoT benefit**: High—Socratic dialogue is inherently multi-step reasoning.

- **Verifiability**: Yes—the symbolic solver verifies the final answer.

**Verdict: SFT warmup + GRPO.** Use the 30 examples for SFT to teach
dialogue format, then GRPO with the symbolic solver as the reward function.
This mirrors the Wordle SFT+GRPO strategy.

**Key Insight:** The three gates are not equally weighted. Verifiability
is the hard gate—without it, GRPO is off the table regardless of label count
or CoT benefit. With verifiability, even 30 labels suffice because they serve
as SFT warmup rather than the primary training signal.

**Answer:**

1.  SFT (abundant labels, pattern matching, no CoT benefit).

2.  DPO (subjective quality, no verifiable reward).

3.  SFT warmup + GRPO (verifiable via symbolic solver, multi-step CoT, sparse labels).
