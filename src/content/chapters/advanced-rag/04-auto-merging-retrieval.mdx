<NarrativeBox title="Where We Are">

Module 3 solved the embedding–synthesis tension for contiguous context
using sentence windows. But what if the relevant information is spread
across *non-adjacent* sections of a document? Auto-merging
retrieval addresses this with a hierarchical approach: instead of
expanding around a single sentence, it merges distant leaf nodes
into their shared parent when enough siblings are relevant.

</NarrativeBox>

# The Context Fragmentation Problem



<Sidenote category="interview">"How do you handle fragmented context in RAG?" is a strong differentiator question. Knowing auto-merging retrieval shows depth beyond the standard techniques.</Sidenote>



<ConceptBox>

**The Fragmentation Problem.**
Standard RAG<Citation src="lewis2020retrieval" /> retrieves individual chunks and
places them into the LLM’s context window. With smaller chunk sizes, you
may get two or more chunks from roughly the same section with
**no guarantees on ordering**.
The LLM receives a disjointed collage of text fragments instead of
coherent passages, hampering its ability to synthesise a comprehensive
answer.

</ConceptBox>



<Term name="Context Fragmentation" los="BEA-4.1">
  A failure mode in standard RAG where the retrieved chunks are disjointed fragments from different parts of a document, lacking logical ordering and coherence. Worse with smaller chunk sizes. Auto-merging retrieval addresses this through hierarchical consolidation.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"Context Fragmentation"} back={"A failure mode in standard RAG where the retrieved chunks are disjointed fragments from different parts of a document, lacking logical ordering and coherence. Worse with smaller chunk sizes. Auto-merging retrieval addresses this through hierarchical consolidation."} los="BEA-4.1" />



Sentence-window retrieval partially solves this by expanding around
each retrieved sentence, but it only captures *contiguous* context.
If the relevant information spans non-adjacent sections (e.g., child 1
and child 4 of a parent), sentence windowing cannot bridge the gap.

# How Auto-Merging Retrieval Works

<PipelineBox title="Pipeline: Auto-Merging Retrieval">

1.  **Parse** — Define a **hierarchy of chunks**:
    smaller child nodes link to bigger parent nodes. Each parent’s
    text equals the combined text of all its children.

2.  **Embed leaf nodes only** — Only the smallest
    (leaf) nodes are embedded and indexed in the vector store.
    Parent and intermediate nodes are stored in a document store.

3.  **Retrieve** — Fetch top-K leaf nodes via
    similarity search (large K due to small leaf size).

4.  **Merge** — If the retrieved children for a given
    parent exceed a **percentage threshold**, replace the
    children with the parent node.

5.  **Re-rank & Synthesise** — Re-rank the merged
    results and feed the top-N to the LLM.

</PipelineBox>



<Term name="Auto-Merging" los="BEA-4.2">
  A retrieval strategy where multiple retrieved child nodes are replaced by their parent node when the number of retrieved children exceeds a threshold (e.g., majority of siblings). Produces more coherent, contiguous context by consolidating related fragments.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"Auto-Merging"} back={"A retrieval strategy where multiple retrieved child nodes are replaced by their parent node when the number of retrieved children exceeds a threshold (e.g., majority of siblings). Produces more coherent, contiguous context by consolidating related fragments."} los="BEA-4.2" />



# The Hierarchical Node Structure

<ConceptBox>

**Example Hierarchy (Three Layers).**
Each level divides by a factor of 4: chunk sizes of
2048 → 512 → 128 tokens.

        Parent (2048 tokens)
        +-- Child (512 tokens)
        |   +-- Leaf (128 tokens)
        |   +-- Leaf (128 tokens)
        |   +-- Leaf (128 tokens)
        |   +-- Leaf (128 tokens)
        +-- Child (512 tokens)
        |   +-- Leaf (128 tokens)
        |   +-- ...
        +-- ...

</ConceptBox>



<Term name="Leaf Node" los="BEA-4.3">
  The smallest chunk in a hierarchical node structure. Only leaf nodes are embedded and indexed for vector search. Their small size (e.g., 128 tokens) enables precise semantic matching. Each leaf stores a relationship to its parent node.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"Leaf Node"} back={"The smallest chunk in a hierarchical node structure. Only leaf nodes are embedded and indexed for vector search. Their small size (e.g., 128 tokens) enables precise semantic matching. Each leaf stores a relationship to its parent node."} los="BEA-4.3" />





<Term name="Parent Node" los="BEA-4.3">
  A larger chunk in the hierarchy whose text equals the concatenation of all its child nodes. Stored in the document store (not embedded). Retrieved dynamically when enough of its children are matched during the merging step.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"Parent Node"} back={"A larger chunk in the hierarchy whose text equals the concatenation of all its child nodes. Stored in the document store (not embedded). Retrieved dynamically when enough of its children are matched during the merging step."} los="BEA-4.3" />



The hierarchy can have two or more levels. The course experiments with both:

| **Structure** | **Layers**                   | **Chunk Sizes**  |
|:--------------|:-----------------------------|:-----------------|
| Two-layer     | Leaf + Parent                | 512 → 2048       |
| Three-layer   | Leaf + Intermediate + Parent | 128 → 512 → 2048 |

# HierarchicalNodeParser



<Term name="HierarchicalNodeParser" los="BEA-4.3">
  A LlamaIndex node parser that creates a tree of nodes at multiple chunk sizes with parent-child relationships. Accepts a list of chunk sizes (from largest to smallest). Returns all nodes at every level, with each child node storing a reference to its parent.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"HierarchicalNodeParser"} back={"A LlamaIndex node parser that creates a tree of nodes at multiple chunk sizes with parent-child relationships. Accepts a list of chunk sizes (from largest to smallest). Returns all nodes at every level, with each child node storing a reference to its parent."} los="BEA-4.3" />





```cypher
from llama_index.node_parser import HierarchicalNodeParser, get_leaf_nodes

# Three-layer hierarchy: 2048 -> 512 -> 128 tokens
node_parser = HierarchicalNodeParser.from_defaults(
    chunk_sizes=[2048, 512, 128]
)
nodes = node_parser.get_nodes_from_documents([document])

# Extract only leaf nodes for embedding
leaf_nodes = get_leaf_nodes(nodes)

# Inspect parent-child relationships
leaf = leaf_nodes[0]
print(leaf.text)  # 128-token chunk
print(leaf.relationships)  # reference to 512-token parent

# The parent's text encompasses all its children
parent = nodes[leaf.relationships["parent"].node_id]
print(len(parent.text))  # ~512 tokens
```





<Sidenote category="warning">The `get_nodes_from_documents` method returns *all* nodes (leaf, intermediate, parent). You must use `get_leaf_nodes` to extract only the leaves for embedding.</Sidenote>



# Building the Auto-Merging Index

<KeyConcept title="Dual-Store Architecture" los="BEA-4.4">

Auto-merging retrieval requires two stores:

- **Vector store**: Contains *only leaf nodes*
  with their embeddings for similarity search.

- **Document store**: Contains *all nodes* (leaf,
  intermediate, parent) for dynamic retrieval during merging.

The vector index has access to the document store through the storage
context, enabling the merging logic to fetch parent nodes on demand.

**When It Breaks:** If the document store is missing parent nodes
(e.g., only leaf nodes were added), the merging step silently fails and
the retriever degrades to standard flat retrieval. Always verify that
`docstore.add_documents(nodes)` receives *all* hierarchy
levels, not just leaves.

</KeyConcept>



```cypher
from llama_index import VectorStoreIndex, StorageContext

# Storage context with in-memory document store
storage_context = StorageContext.from_defaults()
storage_context.docstore.add_documents(nodes)  # ALL nodes

# Service context with LLM, embedding model, node parser
service_context = ServiceContext.from_defaults(
    llm=llm, embed_model=embed_model, node_parser=node_parser
)

# Vector index on LEAF nodes only
auto_merging_index = VectorStoreIndex(
    leaf_nodes,  # Only leaf nodes are embedded
    storage_context=storage_context,
    service_context=service_context,
)
```





<Sidenote category="pattern">The dual-store pattern separates the search index (optimised for similarity) from the content store (optimised for hierarchical traversal). This separation of concerns enables dynamic merging without re-embedding.</Sidenote>





<Sidenote category="warning">Monitor merging frequency in logs. If merging never triggers, increase top-K or reduce leaf chunk size. If it always triggers, the hierarchy may be too coarse.</Sidenote>



# AutoMergingRetriever and Query Engine



<Term name="AutoMergingRetriever" los="BEA-4.4">
  A LlamaIndex retriever that wraps a base retriever and applies hierarchical merging logic. After initial retrieval of leaf nodes, it checks each parent: if a majority of a parent's children were retrieved, all children are replaced with the parent. Requires access to the storage context containing the full node hierarchy.
</Term>
<AnkiCard type="term" front={"AutoMergingRetriever"} back={"A LlamaIndex retriever that wraps a base retriever and applies hierarchical merging logic. After initial retrieval of leaf nodes, it checks each parent: if a majority of a parent's children were retrieved, all children are replaced with the parent. Requires access to the storage context containing the full node hierarchy."} los="BEA-4.4" />



Because leaf nodes have a small chunk size (128 tokens), use a
**large top-K** for initial retrieval, then apply re-ranking
after merging to reduce token usage:

| **Step**          | **Parameter** |                         **Example Value** |
|:------------------|:--------------|------------------------------------------:|
| Initial retrieval | top-K         |                                        12 |
| After merging     | ∼top-K        | ∼10 (depends on merging) |
| After re-ranking  | top-N         |                                         6 |



```cypher
from llama_index.retrievers import AutoMergingRetriever
from llama_index.query_engine import RetrieverQueryEngine
from llama_index.postprocessor import SentenceTransformerRerank

# Auto-merging retriever with large initial top-K
auto_merging_retriever = AutoMergingRetriever(
    auto_merging_index.as_retriever(similarity_top_k=12),
    auto_merging_index.storage_context,
)

# Re-ranker to filter after merging
rerank = SentenceTransformerRerank(
    top_n=6, model="BAAI/bge-reranker-base"
)

# Combine retriever + re-ranker into query engine
auto_merging_engine = RetrieverQueryEngine(
    auto_merging_retriever,
    node_postprocessors=[rerank],
)
```





<Sidenote category="practice">Monitor the merging process in logs: "merging 3 nodes into parent node" confirms the hierarchy is working. If merging never triggers, increase top-K or reduce leaf chunk size.</Sidenote>



# Two-Layer vs. Three-Layer Structures



<Sidenote category="interview">Being able to explain *why* three layers outperform two—smaller leaves enable more precise retrieval and better merging—shows you understand the mechanism, not just the result.</Sidenote>



Anupam Datta demonstrates the impact of hierarchy depth:

## Two-Layer Structure (App 0)

- Leaf: 512 tokens, Parent: 2048 tokens

- Context relevance: notably **low**

- Answer relevance and groundedness: moderate

## Three-Layer Structure (App 1)

- Leaf: 128 tokens, Intermediate: 512 tokens, Parent: 2048 tokens

- Context relevance: **+20% improvement** over two-layer

- Groundedness: considerably higher

- Total cost: ∼**50% lower**

| **Metric** | **Two-Layer** | **Three-Layer** | **Reason** |
|:---|:--:|:--:|:---|
| Context Relevance | Low | +20% | Smaller leaves = more precise retrieval |
| Groundedness | Moderate | Higher | Better context → less compensation |
| Total Cost | Higher | ∼50% lower | Fewer tokens at leaf level |

<ConceptBox>

**Why Three Layers Win.**
The three-layer structure performs better because:

1.  **Smaller leaf nodes** (128 vs. 512) enable more precise
    initial retrieval—the embedding similarity signal is cleaner.

2.  **Better merging** occurs with the finer-grained hierarchy—more
    leaf nodes means more opportunities for sibling overlap.

3.  **Lower cost** results from processing fewer tokens at the
    leaf level during embedding.

</ConceptBox>

# Auto-Merging vs. Sentence-Window Retrieval

<KeyConcept title="Complementary Techniques" los="BEA-4.7">

Sentence-window and auto-merging retrieval are **complementary**,
not competing. Each excels in different scenarios:

| **Scenario** | **Sentence Window** | **Auto-Merging** |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Relevant content is **contiguous** | Works well (expands around the sentence) | Also works |
| Relevant content is **non-contiguous** (e.g., child 1 and child 4) | May miss—windowing only captures adjacent text | **Excels**—merges distant children into parent |

Auto-merging can capture relevant information spread across
**non-adjacent sections** of a document, which sentence windowing
cannot.

**When It Breaks:** Neither technique handles cross-document
retrieval well. If the answer requires combining information from two
separate PDFs (not just two sections of the same PDF), both methods
rely on the base vector search to surface chunks from both documents.

</KeyConcept>

<DecisionTree title="Decision: Sentence Window vs. Auto-Merging">

- **Yes—information flows linearly (narratives, tutorials)**  

- **No—information is scattered (contracts, reports with
  cross-references)**  

- **Well-structured (sections, subsections, clear boundaries)**  

- **Loosely structured (free-form text, conversations)**  

- **Minimal overhead**  

- **Willing to invest in infrastructure**  

</DecisionTree>



<Sidenote category="crossref">Experiment with *both* techniques on your specific documents and evaluate with the RAG triad (Module 2). The best choice depends on your data, not on general rules.</Sidenote>



# Putting It All Together

Jerry Liu encapsulates the complete pipeline in two high-level functions:



```cypher
def build_auto_merging_index(
    documents, llm, embed_model, save_dir,
    chunk_sizes=[2048, 512, 128]
):
    """Build an auto-merging vector index.

    1. HierarchicalNodeParser with specified chunk sizes
    2. ServiceContext with LLM, embedding model, node parser
    3. StorageContext with document store containing ALL nodes
    4. VectorStoreIndex from LEAF nodes only
    """
    node_parser = HierarchicalNodeParser.from_defaults(
        chunk_sizes=chunk_sizes
    )
    nodes = node_parser.get_nodes_from_documents(documents)
    leaf_nodes = get_leaf_nodes(nodes)

    service_context = ServiceContext.from_defaults(
        llm=llm, embed_model=embed_model, node_parser=node_parser
    )
    storage_context = StorageContext.from_defaults()
    storage_context.docstore.add_documents(nodes)

    index = VectorStoreIndex(
        leaf_nodes,
        storage_context=storage_context,
        service_context=service_context,
    )
    index.storage_context.persist(persist_dir=save_dir)
    return index


def get_auto_merging_query_engine(
    index, similarity_top_k=12, rerank_top_n=6
):
    """Create a query engine with auto-merging and re-ranking.

    1. AutoMergingRetriever with storage context
    2. SentenceTransformerRerank for post-merge filtering
    3. RetrieverQueryEngine combining retriever + re-ranker
    """
    retriever = AutoMergingRetriever(
        index.as_retriever(similarity_top_k=similarity_top_k),
        index.storage_context,
    )
    rerank = SentenceTransformerRerank(
        top_n=rerank_top_n, model="BAAI/bge-reranker-base"
    )
    return RetrieverQueryEngine(
        retriever, node_postprocessors=[rerank]
    )
```

# Chapter Summary

Auto-merging retrieval solves the context fragmentation problem by
hierarchically merging child nodes into parent nodes when a majority
of children are retrieved. Only leaf nodes are embedded; parent and
intermediate nodes live in a document store for dynamic merging. A
three-layer hierarchy (128/512/2048 tokens) outperformed a two-layer
hierarchy (512/2048) with +20% context relevance improvement and
∼50% cost reduction, because smaller leaves enable more precise
retrieval and the finer-grained hierarchy produces better merging.
Auto-merging is complementary to sentence-window retrieval—it
captures non-contiguous relevant information that windowing misses.
The choice between techniques depends on your document structure:
use sentence windows for linearly flowing text, auto-merging for
documents with scattered relevant sections.

<KeyConcept title="Hierarchical Merging for Non-Contiguous Context" los="BEA-4.1">

**Core Insight:** Auto-merging retrieval embeds fine-grained leaf nodes
(128 tokens) for precision, then dynamically merges them into coarser parent
nodes when a majority of siblings are retrieved. This captures non-adjacent
relevant information that contiguous sentence windows miss.

**Why It Matters:** Many documents have relevant information scattered
across sections (contracts referencing appendices, papers with methods and
results in different sections). Sentence windows only expand contiguously;
auto-merging consolidates relevant fragments regardless of their position
in the document hierarchy.

**When It Breaks:** The merging threshold (majority of siblings) can be
too aggressive for sparse relevance—if only 1 of 4 siblings is relevant,
merging dilutes precision. Also, the hierarchy is fixed at index time; documents
with irregular structure (tables, mixed formats) may produce suboptimal
hierarchical decompositions.

</KeyConcept>

<Problem title="Hierarchy Structure Calculation" los="BEA-4.3">

A three-layer hierarchy uses chunk sizes \[2048, 512, 128\]. Each parent has
4 children and each intermediate has 4 children. How many leaf nodes does one
root node contain? If a document has 8,192 tokens, how many root nodes are
created?

</Problem>

<Solution>

**Approach:**
**Leaf nodes per root:** 4 × 4 = 16 leaf nodes per root node.

**Root nodes for 8,192 tokens:** 8, 192/2, 048 = 4 root nodes,
each containing 16 leaves.

**Total leaf nodes:** 4 × 16 = 64 leaf nodes, each 128 tokens.

**Key Insight:** Smaller leaves (128 vs. 512) enable more precise
retrieval. The hierarchy provides log<sub>4</sub>(*N*) levels of context expansion
without requiring contiguous proximity.

**Answer:** Smaller leaves (128 vs. 512) enable more precise
retrieval. The hierarchy provides log<sub>4</sub>(*N*) levels of context expansion
without requiring contiguous proximity.

</Solution>

<Problem title="Two-Layer vs. Three-Layer Diagnosis" los="BEA-4.6">

Your two-layer auto-merging system (leaf=512, parent=2048) scores
CR=0.48 on the query “Discuss budgeting for AI projects.” After switching
to three-layer (leaf=128, intermediate=512, parent=2048), CR rises to
0.68. Explain why smaller leaf nodes solved the problem.

</Problem>

<Solution>

**Approach:**
**Root cause:** With 512-token leaves, each leaf averages over ∼2
paragraphs. If the budget discussion spans only 1–2 sentences within a
paragraph, the 512-token leaf dilutes the budget-related signal with
irrelevant surrounding text. The embedding becomes a noisy average that
doesn’t match the query well.

**Why 128-token leaves help:** Smaller leaves isolate the budget-relevant
sentences, producing embeddings that more precisely match the query. When
multiple budget-related leaves across different sections are retrieved, the
merger consolidates them into a coherent 512-token intermediate—capturing
both precision and context.

**Key Insight:** Leaf size is the precision dial. Smaller leaves =
sharper semantic matching. The hierarchy handles context expansion, so there’s
no precision-context tradeoff at the leaf level.

**Answer:** Leaf size is the precision dial. Smaller leaves =
sharper semantic matching.

</Solution>

<Problem title="Technique Selection" los="BEA-4.7">

A research paper has Sections A, B, C, each ∼2,000 tokens. Relevant
information for most queries is within a single section (contiguous). Would
you use sentence-window or auto-merging retrieval? Justify with RAG triad
implications.

</Problem>

<Solution>

**Approach:**
**Answer:** Sentence-window retrieval.

**Reasoning:** When relevant information is contiguous within sections,
sentence-window retrieval excels: it embeds individual sentences for precise
matching, then expands to surrounding sentences for context. The expansion is
contiguous, which perfectly matches the document structure.

Auto-merging would work but is overkill: the hierarchical decomposition adds
complexity (dual stores, merging logic) without benefit, since there’s no
non-contiguous information to consolidate.

**RAG triad implications:**

- **Context relevance:** Both techniques would score similarly, since
  relevant context is within reach of both methods.

- **Groundedness:** Sentence-window may score slightly higher because
  expanded windows provide tighter context (less noise than merged parent chunks).

- **Cost:** Sentence-window has lower indexing and query complexity.

**Key Insight:** Document structure determines technique. Contiguous
relevance → sentence-window. Scattered relevance → auto-merging.

</Solution>

<Vignette title="Retrieval Strategy for Contract Analysis">

\[BEA-4.1, BEA-4.7\]
A legal-AI company builds a RAG system over commercial contracts. Contracts
typically have:

- Definitions (Section 1) referenced throughout

- Payment terms (Section 4) referencing liability limits (Section 8)

- Appendices with schedules referenced by main clauses

The baseline RAG system (512-token chunks) scores CR=0.39 on cross-reference
queries like “What are the payment terms and associated liability limits?”

**Question 1:** Why does baseline chunking fail on cross-reference queries?

**Question 2:** Compare sentence-window (*w* = 3) vs. auto-merging
(3-layer) for this use case. Which is better and why?

**Question 3:** Propose a hybrid approach that combines both techniques.

</Vignette>

<Solution>

**Approach:**
**Q1:** Cross-reference queries require information from multiple
non-adjacent sections (Section 4 + Section 8 + Appendix). A 512-token chunk
captures at most one section. The retriever finds payment terms OR liability
limits, but rarely both—leading to incomplete context and low groundedness.

**Q2:** Auto-merging (3-layer) is superior. Sentence-window expands
*contiguously*—it can expand around the payment clause but cannot reach
the liability section 20 pages away. Auto-merging embeds 128-token leaves
across all sections; when leaves from both Section 4 and Section 8 are
retrieved, they merge into coherent parent nodes providing cross-section
context.

**Q3:** Hybrid approach:

1.  **Auto-merging primary:** 3-layer hierarchy over the full contract
    for cross-reference queries (the common case).

2.  **Sentence-window secondary:** Separate index for definitions
    (Section 1) with *w* = 5, since definitions are self-contained and benefit
    from contiguous expansion.

3.  **Query routing:** Classify queries as “cross-reference” or
    “definition lookup” and route to the appropriate index.

**Key Insight:** Cross-reference queries expose the fundamental limitation of fixed-size chunking: relevant information spans multiple non-adjacent sections that a single chunk cannot capture.

**Answer:** Auto-merging (3-layer hierarchy) outperforms sentence-window for cross-reference queries. It dynamically promotes parent nodes when child matches span sections, capturing payment terms AND liability limits in one merged context.

</Solution>
