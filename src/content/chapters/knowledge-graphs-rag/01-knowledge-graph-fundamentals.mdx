<NarrativeBox title="Where we are">
You've built a basic RAG system: split documents into chunks, embed them, retrieve
by cosine similarity. It works — but it misses *connections*. When a user asks
"who invested in NetApp?", vector search can't traverse from a text chunk to a
company to its investors. **Knowledge graphs store data as interconnected
entities**, so retrieval can follow relationships instead of relying on embedding
distance alone. This chapter establishes the vocabulary and mental model for the
rest of the guide.
</NarrativeBox>

## Why knowledge graphs matter for RAG

<Newthought>In a basic RAG system</Newthought> documents become chunks, chunks
become vectors, and retrieval is nearest-neighbour by cosine similarity. That
finds *semantically similar* text well — but it cannot discover *structural
connections* between entities. Store those chunks in a
[knowledge graph](../glossary#term-knowledge-graph) and a new option opens:
retrieve one chunk, then [**traverse relationships**](../glossary#term-graph-traversal)
to reach connected chunks and entities that similarity search would never surface. <EvidenceTag kind="verified" />

<ConceptBox>
**The key insight.** [Vector similarity search](../glossary#term-vector-similarity-search)
answers *"what text is similar to my question?"* Knowledge-graph traversal answers
*"what entities are connected to this result?"* Combining both gives a RAG system
information neither can surface alone. A vector search finds a chunk about NetApp;
traversal from it follows `PART_OF → Form → FILED → Company → OWNS_STOCK_IN` to
reach the investment firms holding NetApp stock — which no embedding distance
could retrieve.
</ConceptBox>

<Aside category="warning">
Vector search finds *similar* text; graph traversal finds *connected* entities. Conflating these two retrieval modes is a common system-design-interview mistake.
</Aside>

<Aside category="pattern">
Where practitioners disagree — *is a knowledge graph worth the added complexity over plain vector RAG?* One camp says graphs are essential for multi-hop, relationship-heavy questions (investors, org charts, supply chains) that embeddings simply can't answer. The other notes that a graph adds an extra store, a query language (Cypher), and an extraction pipeline — overhead that plain vector RAG avoids, and that many QA workloads never need. The workable rule: reach for a graph when the *questions* are about connections across entities, not when the data merely *has* relationships. This guide builds the graph-RAG case; keep the cost in view.
</Aside>

<SelfCheck q="Name two retrieval strategies a knowledge graph enables beyond vector similarity search, and the kind of question each answers." los="KGR-1.2">
**Graph traversal** (follow typed relationships from a starting node to connected entities — answers "what is *connected* to this?") and **multi-hop expansion** (chain several relationships to reach indirectly-related context — answers "what is connected to the things connected to this?"). Vector search only answers "what text is *similar*?", so the graph adds connection-based retrieval that embedding distance can't.
</SelfCheck>

## The graph data model

<ConceptBox>
**The mental model.** Think of a knowledge graph as a whiteboard diagram that is
also a database. Each circle is a [**node**](../glossary#term-node) (an entity),
each arrow is a [**relationship**](../glossary#term-relationship) (a connection),
and both carry key-value [**properties**](../glossary#term-property). The diagram
*is* the data.
</ConceptBox>

- **Nodes** are data records representing entities. In Cypher text notation they're written in **parentheses**: `(Person)`. Each node carries one or more [**labels**](../glossary#term-label) (e.g. `Person`, `Company`, `Movie`) that group it with similar entities, plus zero or more properties.
- **Relationships** connect two nodes and are themselves rich records: a start and end node, a **type** (`ACTED_IN`, `KNOWS`, `OWNS_STOCK_IN`), a **direction**, and optional properties. Written in **square brackets** with arrows.

```cypher
// Person named Andreas KNOWS Person named Andrew, since 2024
(Person {name: "Andreas"})-[:KNOWS {since: 2024}]->(Person {name: "Andrew"})

// Patterns chain to express richer scenarios:
(Person {name: "Andreas"})-[:TEACHES]->(Course {title: "KG for RAG"})
```

<Aside category="pattern">
"Relationship" is preferred over the graph-theory term "edge" because it carries *richness* — a type, a direction, and its own properties — not just a bare link.
</Aside>

<KeyConcept title="Identity through relationships" los="KGR-1.3">
A node's *role* comes from its relationships, not from extra labels. A `Person`
is an actor because it has an `ACTED_IN` relationship, a director because it has
`DIRECTED`, a writer because it has `WROTE` — no separate `Actor`/`Director`/`Writer`
labels needed. Direction and type *are* the semantics. **When it breaks:** when a
role needs role-specific properties (an actor's per-film `salary` vs a director's
`budget`), relationship-only identity pushes those onto the relationship, which can
get unwieldy at scale. <EvidenceTag kind="verified" />
</KeyConcept>

<SelfCheck q="Explain 'identity through relationships'. Why does a Person node not need separate Actor/Director labels?" los="KGR-1.3">
A node's role is encoded by the *typed relationships* it participates in, not by a label or type field. A `Person` with an `ACTED_IN` relationship *is* an actor; the same node with a `DIRECTED` relationship *is* a director — so adding `Actor`/`Director` labels would be redundant with the relationships and risk a "label explosion." Direction and relationship type carry the semantic meaning.
</SelfCheck>

### Graph-theory equivalences

The vocabulary maps directly onto classic graph theory — interviewers use both,
so know each: <EvidenceTag kind="verified" />

| Graph theory | KG term | Why the KG term is preferred |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vertex | Node | More intuitive for data modelling |
| Edge | Relationship | Conveys richness (type, direction, properties) |
| Graph | Knowledge graph | Emphasises semantic meaning and structure |
| Adjacency | Traversal | Describes *following* relationships through data |

## Knowledge graphs vs tabular storage

The core trade-off is **relationship complexity**, not data size. A relational
database represents relationships with foreign keys and reconstructs them with
JOINs; a graph makes relationships first-class, so multi-hop questions become
*traversals* instead of nested JOINs.

| Dimension | Relational DB | Knowledge graph |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schema | Fixed (DDL required) | Flexible (schema-optional) |
| Relationships | Foreign keys + JOINs | First-class records |
| Multi-hop queries | Expensive (nested JOINs) | Natural (traversal) |
| Vector search | Bolt-on (e.g. pgvector) | Native (vector indexes) |
| Query language | SQL | Cypher |
| Best for | Transactional, tabular | Connected, semantic data |

The cost intuition: a $d$-hop traversal touches roughly $O(k^d)$ nodes where $k$
is the average fan-out per node — but it only walks the *local* neighbourhood,
whereas the SQL equivalent needs $d$ self-JOINs over the *whole* table.

<WorkedExample title="Knowledge graph or relational database?" los="KGR-1.4">
**Problem.** A customer-support system has 10M tickets, each assigned to exactly
one agent and one customer, with a status field; you need "find similar past
tickets." KG or relational? Justify on schema flexibility, relationship
complexity, and query pattern.

**Reasoning.**
- *Schema flexibility:* the ticket schema is stable (ticket/agent/customer/status); new fields are a cheap `ALTER TABLE`. No KG advantage.
- *Relationship complexity:* each ticket has *one* agent and *one* customer — simple foreign keys, no many-to-many or multi-hop. No KG advantage.
- *Query pattern:* "find similar tickets" is *semantic similarity*, best served by a vector index, not traversal.

**Answer.** Use a **relational database** (e.g. PostgreSQL + pgvector). The data is
tabular with simple relationships and the real need is similarity, not connection.
A knowledge graph would add complexity without benefit. The lesson: choose the KG
for *relationship complexity*, not for novelty — when relationships are simple
look-ups, relational + vector is simpler and battle-tested.
</WorkedExample>

<SelfCheck q="On which single axis does the knowledge-graph-vs-relational decision most turn, and which way does each side point?" los="KGR-1.4">
**Relationship complexity.** If the data is highly interconnected with many-to-many or multi-hop relationships (and you query *across* them), the graph wins — traversal stays cheap where SQL JOINs explode. If relationships are simple foreign keys and the workload is tabular or pure similarity, a relational database (plus a vector index) is simpler and sufficient. Schema flexibility and native vector+graph queries are secondary tilts toward the graph.
</SelfCheck>

## A first taste of Cypher

[Cypher](../glossary#term-cypher) is Neo4j's declarative, pattern-matching query
language: you *draw* the pattern you want and it returns every matching subgraph.
The notation mirrors the whiteboard — `()` for nodes, `[]` for relationships, `->`
for direction. <EvidenceTag kind="verified" />

```cypher
// Find all people who acted in a movie
MATCH (p:Person)-[:ACTED_IN]->(m:Movie)
RETURN p.name, m.title
```

Read it as: *find a `Person` connected to a `Movie` by an `ACTED_IN` relationship,
and return their names.* The pattern syntax is the graph structure, which makes
Cypher queries self-documenting (Module 2 is a full Cypher tutorial; Module 7 has
an LLM generate Cypher automatically).

<WorkedExample title="Model a domain as a graph" los="KGR-1.1">
**Problem.** Model a university catalogue: professors teach courses, students
enrol in courses, courses belong to departments, some professors advise students.
Give node labels, relationship types, and the Cypher for "Dr. Smith teaches
Machine Learning 101 in the CS department." Can a TA be modelled by relationships
alone?

**Reasoning.** Entities → labels with properties; verbs → typed directed
relationships.

```cypher
(smith:Professor {name: "Dr. Smith"})-[:TEACHES]->
(ml101:Course {title: "Machine Learning 101"})-[:BELONGS_TO]->
(cs:Department {name: "CS"})
```

Labels: `Professor` (name, email), `Student` (name, studentId), `Course` (title,
code, credits), `Department` (name). Relationships: `TEACHES`, `ENROLLED_IN`,
`BELONGS_TO`, `ADVISES`.

**Answer.** Yes — a **TA** is a `Student` with *both* an `ENROLLED_IN` and an
`ASSISTS` relationship to a course; no separate `TA` label is needed. That's
"identity through relationships" again: model a role by the relationships present,
not by adding labels, to avoid the label-explosion anti-pattern.
</WorkedExample>

<CompletionProblem id="cypher-pattern" title="Write the Cypher pattern" level="completion">
Express, in Cypher text notation, a `Person` named **"Alice"** who `WORKS_AT` a
`Company` named **"Acme"** since **2020** (the `since` is a property of the
relationship).

**Your turn — fill the blanks:**
- Nodes go in `___` and relationships in `___`; direction is shown with `___`.
- Pattern: `(alice:Person {___})-[:WORKS_AT {___}]->(acme:Company {___})`

<Fragment slot="solution">
- Nodes in **parentheses** `()`, relationships in **square brackets** `[]`, direction with an **arrow** `->`.
- `(alice:Person {name: "Alice"})-[:WORKS_AT {since: 2020}]->(acme:Company {name: "Acme"})`

The `since: 2020` lives on the *relationship* (inside the `[]`), not the node — relationship properties describe the *connection* (when Alice started), which is exactly the kind of richness that makes "relationship" more than a graph-theory "edge."
</Fragment>
</CompletionProblem>

<SelfCheck q="In Cypher text notation, what encloses a node, what encloses a relationship, and how is direction shown? Write the pattern for a Person who DIRECTED a Movie." los="KGR-1.6">
Nodes are in parentheses `()`, relationships in square brackets `[:TYPE]`, and direction with an arrow `->` (or `<-`). Pattern: `(p:Person)-[:DIRECTED]->(m:Movie)`. Labels follow a colon inside the node; properties go in `{}` on either a node or a relationship.
</SelfCheck>

## Where knowledge graphs are used

<PipelineBox title="Knowledge graphs in production">
1. **Web search** (Google, Bing) — the knowledge cards showing a person's birth date, films, and related people are graph look-ups that aggregate properties and relationships into a summary.
2. **E-commerce / recommendations** (Amazon, Netflix) — "customers who bought X also bought Y" traverses purchase relationships; multi-hop preferences fall out of the graph.
3. **Financial analysis** (SEC, Bloomberg) — investments, corporate structures, and filings form a natural graph. This guide uses SEC Form 10-K and Form 13 filings as its dataset (Modules 4–7).
4. **Enterprise RAG** — document chunks linked to teams, projects, and concepts give context-aware retrieval that respects organisational structure.
</PipelineBox>

<SelfCheck q="Give two real-world uses of knowledge graphs outside RAG, and what the graph encodes in each." los="KGR-1.5">
Two of: **web-search knowledge cards** (entities like people/companies linked by typed relationships, aggregated into a summary panel); **e-commerce recommendations** (purchase/view relationships traversed for "also bought" suggestions); **financial analysis** (companies, investors, and filings as connected entities). In each, the graph encodes *entities and the typed relationships between them*, enabling look-ups and traversals that tabular storage handles awkwardly.
</SelfCheck>

## Summary

A knowledge graph stores data as **nodes** and **relationships**, both carrying
**properties**; **labels** group nodes, and relationship **type + direction**
encode semantic meaning. That structure enables **traversal-based retrieval** that
complements vector similarity — surfacing connections embeddings can't. **Cypher**
expresses queries as the very patterns you'd draw on a whiteboard. Reach for a
graph when your questions are about *connections across entities*; for simple
tabular look-ups, relational storage is simpler.
